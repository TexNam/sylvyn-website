Context

Why Scotland

Scotland generates more clean power than the grid can always move. That constraint is a cost today — and a rare opening for power-hungry compute.

Paying to turn power off

Scotland’s renewables are running ahead of the wires that carry them south. In 2024, roughly £394 million was paid to turn wind generation down; in 2025, about £382 million — almost all of that volume Scottish.

Zoom out to the system operator’s books: Great Britain spent £1.7 billion on thermal constraint costs in financial year 2024/25, with Scotland the main congestion driver. There is no clean Scotland-only official invoice — the geography of the problem is clear anyway.

Clean power exists. Too often it cannot reach demand.

REF Balancing Mechanism tracker · NESO 2025 Annual Balancing Costs Report (GB thermal constraints — not a Scotland-only invoice)

Nodes without a buyer

When generation is constrained and demand is elsewhere, power-ready locations sit underused. The grid pays generators to stand down while hyperscale and AI loads hunt for firm, locatable capacity.

Scotland already produced 38.5 TWh of renewable electricity in 2025 — a record — with about 18.4 GW of renewables online by mid-2026. The bottleneck is not “enough wind.” It is matching surplus power to real load, behind or beside the constraint.

Idle power is wasted value. Locating compute where the electrons already are turns constraint into advantage.

Put the load where the power is

Sylvyn develops data-centre and associated energy infrastructure in Scotland so institutional partners can site demand against that surplus — regional capacity, UK sovereign posture, grid-positive by design.

We do not ask the public site to carry campus pins or capacity ladders. Under NDA, the land, power pathway, and development programme sit in the pack. On the open web, the case is simpler: Scotland’s renewables surplus and constraint costs are real; putting compute next to that power is the rational response.

Scotland’s renewables, in numbers

~18.4 GW Renewables online mid-2026 — ≈11 GW onshore, 4.3 GW offshore, 1.7 GW hydro, under 1 GW solar.
38.5 TWh Renewable electricity generated in Scotland in 2025.
~£380–394m Recent annual wind turn-down (mostly Scottish volume). GB thermal constraints £1.7bn in FY2024/25 — Scotland the main driver.
~99 GW Planning pipeline mid-2026 — roughly 30 GW offshore wind, 18 GW onshore wind, 33 GW battery.
Policy ambitions Onshore 20 GW by 2030. Offshore: older 8–11 GW by 2030 under review; up to 40 GW new by 2040 (Jan 2026 update). Solar proposed 4–6 GW by 2030. Nuclear: Torness only (not renewable).

Figures sourced from Scottish Government Energy Statistics, DESNZ regional capacity, NESO, and REF as dated. Ambitions are policy goals; delivery depends on consenting, grid, and markets. The £1.7bn figure is a GB thermal-constraint total, not a Scotland-only invoice.
